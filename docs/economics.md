# Economics

## Plausible advantages

- Ground-mounted electric drives avoid transporting a tractor-sized engine
  through the field.
- One carrier can use multiple tools, improving utilization of the positioning
  infrastructure.
- Grid, solar, storage, and regenerative-drive options can be evaluated on the
  same electrical architecture.
- Long operating windows may reduce dependence on a human driver.
- Plant-specific applications can reduce material use when sensing and
  targeting are reliable.

## Costs that must not be hidden

Motors and cable are not the entire system. Towers, foundations, certified
structures, pulleys, weatherproof winches, safety systems, redundant sensing,
service infrastructure, maintenance, crop damage, downtime, and skilled support
may dominate capital and operating costs.

## Throughput ceiling

Plant-level work scales with the number of plants and task time:

```text
pass time = plants × (travel time + task time) / simultaneous tools
```

At 50,000 plants and 10 seconds per plant, one tool requires almost 139 hours
before travel is counted. A large carrier with many arms adds mass and cable
load. Multiple small end effectors sharing infrastructure, as proposed by
CAFEs, is an important alternative to evaluate.

## First market hypothesis

The earliest credible application is more likely a high-value, labor-intensive
crop or research setting than commodity grain. Candidate environments include
berries, tomatoes, nursery production, seed plots, and phenotyping facilities.

## Required comparison

The economic model must compare at least:

- capital recovery;
- energy and consumables;
- labor and supervision;
- maintenance and availability;
- operations completed per weather window;
- yield and quality effects;
- avoided equipment and field traffic; and
- cost per successful plant operation.
